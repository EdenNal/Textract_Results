\begin{abstract}
Most historical epidemiological data remain undigitized. This paper evaluates automated table extraction as an alternative to manual data entry, assessing \emph{Amazon Textract} on a large dataset of historical infectious disease records: extraction accuracy, error characteristics, and efficiency relative to manual transcription. Cell-level agreement was evaluated on the 1956 tables (53{,}872 evaluated cells): content-only agreement between the automated and manually transcribed data was 88.5\% overall and 92.9\% among cells numeric in the manual reference transcription (a secondary figure of 95.6\% obtains if the denominator is instead restricted to cells numeric on both sides). Downstream epidemiological comparisons (disease peaks, growth rates, cross-correlations) span all three years of the dataset, 1956--1958. Most residual cell mismatches had a Levenshtein distance of one or two; larger discrepancies were concentrated in rows affected by disease-name or row-alignment failures, and differences in the downstream epidemiological summaries were generally modest. Extraction runtime was short relative to an extrapolated manual-entry estimate, but total person-time, including post-processing, was not measured; the post-processing time we did record was a small proportion of the total time previously required for the whole task. This paper illustrates the potential and limitations of a human-in-the-loop workflow for digitizing historical tabular data of this kind.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Weekly notifiable-disease reports have been collected in printed form for well over a century, but most remain undigitized and unavailable for computational analysis. Records that have been converted to machine-readable form have typically relied on manual transcription -- accurate, but slow enough to constrain which archives are digitized at all, and not itself error-free (\cref{sec:NDD} documents an instance where the manual transcription used in this study contained an error that the automated extraction did not). Automated table extraction is an obvious alternative, but for a researcher fitting models to the resulting series, the relevant question is not how well a tool reads cells in the abstract: it is whether the epidemiological quantities computed from the extracted data match those from a trusted transcription, and, where they do not, whether the discrepancies can be detected without one.

Software that converts images of tables into machine-readable data, such as comma-separated value (CSV) files, typically decomposes the problem into distinct tasks, though different authors characterize them differently \cite{zhong2020image}. We organize our work around three tasks:
\begin{description}
    \item[Table detection:] Locating tables in documents.
    \item[Table structure recognition:] Parsing the row and column layout of tables.
    \item[Content recognition:] Identifying the content of table cells and headers.
\end{description}
Table detection and layout are surprisingly difficult for computers given how easy they are for humans \cite{hu2002evaluating}, a difficulty rooted in decades of OCR research \cite{Mori1992} and still evident in widely used open-source engines such as Tesseract \cite{TessOverview}. Modern document-AI systems -- including large vision-language models applied directly to spreadsheets and tables -- continue to report substantial failure modes on structurally irregular inputs even on contemporary, cleanly scanned material \cite{xia2024vision,Sahasrabudhe2025}, a limitation that compounds on historical documents, where period typography, physical degradation, and non-standard layouts are the norm. Software that produces usable results -- data similar enough to the scanned document to support similar downstream conclusions -- must succeed at all three tasks reasonably well; a single under-performing sub-task is often enough to compromise the result, as we discuss in \cref{sec:NDD}.

Historical health-surveillance archives are a natural and under-served target for this technology. Project Tycho is the most developed prior effort of this kind, digitizing over a century of United States notifiable-disease reports \cite{van2013contagious,van2018project}; Canada's own notifiable-disease surveillance system has a comparably long paper record that, until recently, remained largely undigitized \cite{sockett1996communicable,totten2019updates}. Largely absent from this literature, and from table-extraction benchmarks generally, is evaluation against an independent transcription with downstream, analysis-level epidemiological quantities -- not only cell-level accuracy -- as the endpoint: benchmark accuracy on curated corpora does not say whether the peak timing, growth rate, or cross-correlation an epidemiologist computes from an extracted series would match a trusted transcription, or whether the discrepancies could be caught without one. Reference transcriptions are also conventionally treated as an infallible standard against which extraction is scored; as we show in \cref{sec:NDD}, they can themselves contain errors that a careful comparison can surface.

Using 156 weekly tables from the Canadian Disease Incidence Dataset (CANDID; 1956--1958) \cite{CANDID}, we ask: how closely does a documented, staged Textract-based extraction and post-processing workflow reproduce an independently produced manual transcription at the cell level (evaluated here for the 1956 tables); which classes of error survive each processing stage; and how well are standard epidemiological summaries -- peak timing, peak size, initial growth rate, and between-disease cross-correlation -- preserved before and after targeted human review, for the disease pairs we examine? A useful table-extraction tool must perform all three tasks above while balancing speed, accuracy, and cost against manual entry: extraction time plus correction time should be smaller than the time to do the whole task manually, and no more expensive. Even a tool that scored perfectly on these criteria would not obviate a researcher's own reading of the results \cite{STADLER2024108386} -- verifying a workflow like this one still requires a baseline of human time and attention.

Amazon Textract, the extraction service used throughout this study, is a cloud-based machine learning service that recognizes table, form, and key-value structure in scanned documents, in contrast to OCR tools that read only raw text; it is accessed either through a browser interface or programmatically via an API (\cref{sec:data-extraction} gives implementation details).

\section{Data extraction and Post-Processing} \label{sec:data-extraction}

This section summarizes the process of extracting data from a scanned table to a CSV file, and the post-processing needed to achieve usable results. The basic extraction procedure does not depend on the specific dataset and applies to any PDF repository of files (dataset details are in \cref{sec:NDDdetails}); further details on Textract are in Amazon's official documentation \cite{awsdocumentation}.
For running AWS Textract and the provided extraction script, see \cref{sec:supp}.

\subsection{API Call to Textract}
AWS Textract can be accessed programmatically via API calls with any popular high-level programming language; we use Python, via AWS's official SDK, \texttt{boto3}. A researcher calls Textract's \texttt{AnalyzeDocument} endpoint and receives a flat, nested JSON structure of nested \texttt{BLOCK} objects (tables, cells, and words, each with a confidence score) that must be reassembled into the row/column structure a human would recognize; the full object structure is given in \hyperref[sec:supp]{Supplementary Material} (S3). Once reassembled, this process can be applied to multiple tables at once by iterating through a repository of scanned table files and returning the outputs in separate CSV files.

\subsection{Post-Processing}\label{sec:postprocessing}
Though it is trivial for humans to distinguish cell values from headers, Textract relies on an OCR engine and does not differentiate between the two: headers are not explicitly labelled, merged cells rely on \texttt{RowSpan/ColumnSpan}, and column meaning must be inferred by position. Post-processing is therefore essential for meaningful analysis.

Our post-processing normalized the cell data to match the original PDF tables' structure and cell types: trimming and removing whitespace, and converting cell values into strings.

Recognizing header values versus cell contents depends on the specifics of each dataset; we used the proportion of cell values in a row that were strings, and, through manual inspection of the original PDF dataset, labelled rows with more than 50\% string content as headers. This proved successful: headers were removed correctly in almost every extracted table, with one exception caught and corrected manually.

Another major issue was rows shifted by one cell value, which invalidates an entire row's comparison against the manual data, especially for numerical values. This is fairly easy to detect once comparing cells, so a step was added to check row length against the row above it; this improved results when matching against the manual data (further described in \cref{sec:measuresAccuracy}).

Cells were also corrected to known values based on the original dataset via dataset-specific coding, described in \cref{sec:hardcoding}.



\section{Dataset Criteria and Selection}

This section describes the dataset used to assess the quality of Textract-extracted data. Beyond the general tool-selection criteria in \cref{sec:intro} (accuracy, time, and cost relative to manual entry), the dataset had to satisfy one further requirement: it had to already have a manual transcription, so automated extraction could be checked directly against human data entry.


\subsection{Canadian Disease Incidence Dataset}\label{sec:NDDdetails}

The Canadian Disease Incidence Dataset (CANDID) is a summary of notifiable-disease cases in Canada, by province and territory, maintained by the Dominion Bureau of Statistics (the predecessor of Statistics Canada) \cite{CANDID}. The data was stored in a fixed, typed template, so most cell contents are easily legible to humans.

We chose this dataset chiefly because it had already been independently transcribed by hand \cite{CANDID}, enabling the accuracy measures in \cref{sec:measuresAccuracy}; as detailed there, we treat that manual transcription as a reference for comparison, not a verified gold standard. We also chose it for the qualities of the table itself, described below.

We extracted data from the tables covering disease reports from 1956 to 1958, three years in total. There is one table per week, with columns for each province and territory recognized at the time plus country totals; each province's columns are further split into cases reported for the current week, the previous week, and the median of the previous 5 years.

Each row corresponds to a specific disease, 28 rows in total. Most rows correspond to a single disease (e.g.\ the first row, Chickenpox), but some diseases span multiple rows for additional data -- e.g.\ row 17 splits Tuberculosis into Pulmonary and Non-Pulmonary. See Week 1 of CANDID in \cref{fig:NDD1}.

The table in \cref{fig:NDD1} is easily interpretable by a human observer, but poses several problems for an OCR engine such as Textract. Common problems in the cell structure and content, typical of many historical data documents, include (and lead to the problematic results in \cref{sec:NDD}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item Merged cells: many rows contain merged cells (e.g.\ the Canada case totals, the province names, and the cumulative-total header), making row lengths inconsistent across the table.
    \item Header values for the current week, previous week, and median are printed vertically, while the rest of the table is printed horizontally.
    \item Missing weekly data is marked ``..''; weeks with no cases are marked ``-''.
    \item Footnote markers: cells may carry a parenthetical number (1--6) referring to a footnote on data collection or provenance. For example, ``5(5)'' for P.E.I.\ under report week, row ``Scarlet Fever'', means 5 cases, with footnote (5) reading ``Scarlet Fever includes cases of streptococcal sore throat.''
    \item ``The situation in the United States,'' printed at the bottom of each week's table, is not in tabular format and so was not processed by Textract (a correct table-detection outcome, \cref{sec:intro}) -- but is valuable information the tool did not capture.
\end{itemize}




\section{Statistical Measures} \label{sec:measuresAccuracy}

\subsection{Testing Accuracy of Textract Data Compared to Manually Entered Data}
To assess the accuracy of the CANDID data digitized by Textract, we compared it with the corresponding data digitized by humans in McMaster's Theoretical Biology group, using several metrics.

We refer to this human-digitized dataset as the \emph{reference transcription} rather than a verified gold standard. It is an independently produced transcription, not a ground truth: like any manual entry process, it is itself subject to error, and we do not assume by definition that it is correct. Where possible, disagreements between the two sources are adjudicated against the original source scan rather than resolved in the reference transcription's favour (see \cref{sec:hardcoding} for an example in which this adjudication instead identified an error in the reference transcription).

\subsubsection{Simple Metrics}
The most straightforward metric, the \emph{exact matched-cell proportion}, is the percentage of cells from the Textract dataset exactly matching the reference transcription -- not a measure of cells correctly digitized, but of cells whose content \emph{and} location (row and column index) are both equivalent. We applied this metric to the raw extracted data, then again as the data became progressively more processed through the methods in \cref{sec:postprocessing}.

\paragraph{Inclusion rule.} We define the evaluated cell set reproducibly, following the implementation in \texttt{postprocessing/combine.ipynb}: each cleaned weekly CSV pair (\texttt{data/textract\_cleaned/1956}, \texttt{data/manual\_cleaned/1956}) is compared position-by-position over the overlapping row and column range, i.e.\ \texttt{min(n\_rows)} by \texttt{min(n\_cols)} between the two files for that week; any rows or columns beyond that overlap (e.g.\ from a length mismatch between the two sources) fall outside this range and are not included in the denominator. Merged-cell handling, header/data-row labelling, footnote-marker treatment, and repeated row indices are resolved upstream during the cleaning stage (\cref{sec:postprocessing}) that produces the \texttt{*\_cleaned} inputs, not by the comparison script itself; the comparison operates on whatever string is present in each cleaned cell. Blank cells and ``\texttt{-}''/``\texttt{..}''/``\texttt{...}'' placeholders are retained and compared as literal strings. A cell counts toward the primary numeric denominator in \cref{tab:pipeline} (n=28,255) if the reference (manual) value consists only of digits with optional thousands separators, regardless of what Textract produced for that cell. A cell counts toward the secondary, numeric-both-sources denominator (n=26,069) only if the Textract value is also numeric by the same rule; cells where either side is empty or a placeholder are excluded from both numeric denominators. The row-shift tolerance (\cref{tab:pipeline}, Stage 2) checks whether a Textract cell matches the reference either in its original column or in the adjacent column one position to the right.

\paragraph{Cells excluded by the overlap restriction.} Because the comparison in \cref{tab:pipeline} scores only the overlapping row/column region of each weekly table pair (the inclusion rule above), it does not score every cell Textract produced. Across the 52 cleaned 1956 tables, the evaluated overlap totals 53{,}872 cells, while the full cleaned Textract bounding grids (\texttt{data/textract\_cleaned/1956}, summed row $\times$ column extent per file) total 61{,}116 cells -- so 7{,}244 Textract cells (11.9\% of the full grid) fall outside the evaluated overlap and are not scored by any of the agreement figures in this paper. These excluded cells are not automatically 7{,}244 additional errors: they can include border/padding rows introduced by the cleaning step, repeated header rows, or other structural artefacts of aligning two independently formatted tables, rather than substantive disease-count cells. We report this count for transparency but have not manually audited the 7{,}244 excluded cells to determine how many are non-trivial content versus padding; this is a scope limitation of the reported accuracy figures (see \cref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection{String Differences and Levenshtein Distance} \label{sec:levdist}
To quantify discrepancies between cells with extracted string and their manually entered counterparts, we compute string-level similarity metrics, with a primary focus on Levenshtein distance \cite{Levenshtein1965BinaryCC}. Levenshtein distance measures the minimum number of single-character edit operations -- insertions, deletions, or substitutions -- required to transform one string into another; a distance of zero indicates an exact match, while larger values reflect increasing divergence. The standard dynamic-programming recurrence for computing it is given in \hyperref[sec:supp]{Supplementary Material} (S4).

The dynamic-programming formulation above follows the general string-to-string correction framework of Wagner and Fischer \cite{wagner1974string}. In practice, Levenshtein distance is often normalized by string length to enable fair comparison across tokens of different sizes. This is particularly important when evaluating OCR output, where short strings (e.g., years or counts) are disproportionately sensitive to single-character errors.


\subsection{Epidemiologically Significant Statistics Derived from Textract Data}

\subsubsection{Row and Column Sums}
Aggregate counts obtained by summing across diseases or locations are often needed, and where the original documents provide these sums, they offer a valuable consistency check: summing values across a row or down a column and confirming the result matches the reported total flags entries that were missed, duplicated, or mistyped. Errors in both the extracted sums and incidence values could in principle cancel out, but this is unlikely; discrepancies flag rows or columns for closer review, letting errors be identified and corrected before downstream analysis.

\subsubsection{Cross-Correlation Between Diseases}
Computing cross-correlation between two disease time series measures how strongly their patterns move together over time, and whether changes in one tend to occur before, after, or alongside changes in the other -- e.g.\ whether Measles peaks are followed by Chickenpox peaks one or two weeks later. Rather than asking whether two diseases are correlated in a broad sense, cross-correlation examines their relationship across different time shifts, comparing the standardized values of one series with shifted versions of the other to produce a correlation value at each lag.

Ordinary correlation measures the linear association between two random variables $X$ and $Y$ as the normalized covariance, a quantity in $[-1,1]$ giving the strength and direction of linear dependence. For time series $\{x_t\}$ and $\{y_t\}$, ordinary correlation compares values at the same time index:
\[
\rho_{xy}(0) = 
\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x_t, y_t)}{\sigma_x \, \sigma_y}.
\]

Cross-correlation extends this idea by introducing a time shift, called the lag. The cross-covariance at lag $k$ is
\[
\gamma_{xy}(k) = \mathrm{Cov}(x_t, y_{t-k}),
\]
and the cross-correlation function (CCF) is
\[
\rho_{xy}(k) = 
\frac{\gamma_{xy}(k)}{\sigma_x \, \sigma_y}.
\]

Thus $\rho_{xy}(k)$ is the correlation between $x_t$ and a shifted version of $y_t$; varying $k$ measures linear dependence across different time offsets.

\paragraph{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $k = 0$: simultaneous association.
    \item $k > 0$: $x$ leads $y$ by $k$ time units.
    \item $k < 0$: $y$ leads $x$ by $|k|$ time units.
\end{itemize}



We compute the CCF, $\rho_{xy}(k)$, for $x$ and $y$ referring to two different diseases reported in the same period -- once from the manually curated data and once from the automatically extracted data. If the two resulting CCFs are sufficiently similar, this supports the automatic extraction being accurate enough for epidemiologically meaningful inferences.

We assess similarity across all lags $k$ by comparing (i) the lag of maximum correlation, (ii) the magnitude of peak correlations, and (iii) the overall shape of the CCF curves, quantified by the difference
\[
\Delta(k) = \rho_{xy}^{\mathrm{T}}(k) - \rho_{xy}^{\mathrm{M}}(k),
\]
where superscripts M and T denote manual and Textract data. Similarity in magnitude, peak location, and overall structure suggests the extracted data preserves the dependence structure of the manual dataset.


\subsubsection{Other Time Series Statistics}

Another verification tactic is to filter the data by disease -- or more generally, by category or type -- and examine each subset independently; this makes it easier to spot values inconsistent with the typical range for that group, with outliers identified by scatterplots, boxplots, interquartile range, or standard-deviation cutoffs. Using more than one approach reduces the risk of missing meaningful anomalies or over-flagging valid but extreme observations, and helps distinguish true data-entry errors from legitimate but rare values.

Of the statistics an epidemiologist would naturally want, the \emph{peak height} (the largest observed value) and \emph{peak time} (when it occurs) help predict the intensity of an outbreak.

\paragraph{Peak-detection rule (\crefrange{table:measles_growthrate_results}{tab:growthrateschickenpox}).} Because a raw weekly maximum is sensitive to isolated OCR spikes and single-week noise, peaks are located from a locally smoothed series rather than the raw weekly values: within each fitting window, we fit a local quadratic regression (LOESS, span $0.4$, degree 2) to weekly cases against week index, and report the week and fitted value at which this smoothed curve is maximized. Ties in the smoothed maximum resolve to the earliest week in the window. Weeks with a missing time index or missing case count are dropped prior to smoothing rather than imputed; a window is not fit if fewer than 5 non-missing weeks remain. We separately checked whether any reported peak week (Measles: weeks 64 and 126; Chickenpox: weeks 64, 112, and 114) coincides with a week flagged by the row-sum validation of \cref{sec:hardcoding}: none can be checked, because that validation covers only the 52 weeks of 1956, while every reported peak here falls in 1957 or 1958. This is itself a limitation of the current validation coverage, not evidence that the reported peaks are error-free.

Testing the growth rate is also important, as it is a preliminary statistic researchers want to know about an epidemic: estimating the initial growth rate of a disease time series quantifies how quickly cases increase during the early phase of an outbreak, giving a concise measure of epidemic speed that supports comparison across locations or time periods. The early phase of an outbreak is typically well-approximated by exponential growth, where the expected number of cases increases proportionally to the current number of cases. The initial exponential growth rate, denoted $r$ throughout this paper, is particularly important as it yields the epidemic doubling time ($\frac{\ln{2}}{r}$); in principle it can also derive quantities such as the basic reproduction number $\mathcal{R}_0$ \cite{WallLips07}, though we do not compute $\mathcal{R}_0$ here -- we mention it only as context for why $r$ is of independent epidemiological interest, not only an intermediate step toward the doubling time.

Methods for estimating the initial exponential growth rate \cite{Ma2014,JaganRpkg} typically exploit the fact that if incidence grows exponentially, cumulative incidence does too. Since datasets are often observed at substantial intervals (e.g., weekly), methods using ``interval incidence'' (the difference between cumulative incidence at observation times) are more robust. The growth rate also gives the doubling time, a statistic that is easier to interpret numerically:
The exponential growth rate \(r\) is related to the doubling time $T_{\mathrm{d}}$ by
\[
T_{\mathrm{d}} = \frac{\ln 2}{r}
\]

\paragraph{Model specification (\crefrange{table:measles_growthrate_results}{tab:growthrateschickenpox}, \crefrange{fig:growthratesm}{fig:growthrateschickenpox}).} Growth rates and doubling times for Measles and Chickenpox were estimated with \texttt{epigrowthfit} 0.15.5 \cite{JaganRpkg} in R 4.5.1, using \texttt{egf\_model(curve = "logistic")} with all other model arguments left at their package defaults (negative-binomial dispersion, no day-of-week effect -- not applicable, since our data are weekly rather than daily -- and no excess-cases term). Every top-level model parameter, including $\log(r)$, used the default intercept-only formula, so a single growth rate $r$ was estimated jointly from both fitting windows for a given disease and data source, rather than a separate rate per window; \cref{sec:NDD} reports what this implies for the two-window presentation in \crefrange{fig:growthratesm}{fig:growthrateschickenpox}. Confidence intervals are Wald intervals computed on the $\log(r)$ scale via \texttt{confint.egf()} and back-transformed. The two fitting windows (week 35--64 and week 95--126, bracketing the two annual epidemic waves visible in the incidence curves) were chosen by visual inspection of the manually entered time series and then applied unchanged to the corresponding Textract series for the same disease; windows were not re-chosen from the Textract data itself. Weeks with a missing time index or case count inside a window are dropped prior to fitting, matching the treatment used for peak detection above.

\section{Analyzing the results from the Canadian Disease Incidence Dataset} \label{sec:NDD}
This section explores the accuracy of table extraction on the Canadian Disease Incidence Dataset (CANDID).


\subsection{Comparison to Manually Entered Data}
\subsubsection{Simple Metrics}\label{sec:simple}
The cell-agreement analysis in this section covers the 1956 CANDID tables only, not the full 1956--1958 (156-table) dataset described in \cref{sec:NDDdetails}. It was computed with the evaluation script in \texttt{postprocessing/combine.ipynb}, run over the 52 weekly tables for 1956 (53{,}872 evaluated cells in total; 26{,}069 of these hold numeric values on both the Textract and reference sides). When comparing values cell by cell, the percentage of cells between the raw Textract data and the manually entered reference transcription that were equivalent (both content and position, i.e.\ strict string equality; see \cref{sec:measuresAccuracy}) was 74.0\% across all evaluated cells, or 71.9\% among the 28{,}255 cells numeric in the manual reference (77.9\% if instead restricted to the 26{,}069 cells numeric on both sides). \subsubsection{String Differences}
Using Python's NLTK library, we compute the Levenshtein distance between extracted and reference strings to find the distribution of edit distances among mismatched entries, from cells differing by a single character to cells with little resemblance to the reference value, and the average distance among entries with an actual error. Recall from \cref{sec:simple} that content-only agreement is 88.5\% across all evaluated cells, distinct from the strict content-and-position agreement of 74.0\%, in which a cell's content may be correct but extracted to the wrong location. Below, ``mismatched'' cells are those failing even the content-only criterion (\texttt{Any\_Equality = False}), i.e.\ the residual disagreements not resolved by any processing stage in \cref{tab:pipeline}. The average Levenshtein distance among these cells is approximately 1.84; the modal distance is 2 (51.7\% of mismatched cells), with 1 the next most common (38.1\%). \cref{fig:Ldist} shows the proportion of cell contents per Levenshtein distance.

    \label{fig:Ldist}
\end{figure}

The most common Levenshtein distances are 1 or 2, as expected, consistent with \cref{sec:simple}'s finding that most cell contents differ by a single digit or special character. Of the 1956 tables, 6{,}177 of 53{,}872 evaluated cells (11.5\%) are true extraction errors (mistakes not resolved through post-processing); of these, 38.1\% have a Levenshtein distance of 1 and 51.7\% have a distance of 2.

The distance drops off steeply after 2, with a thin tail out to a maximum of 25 -- likely cases where the OCR engine confuses a blurry part of a number, or the table structure or scan quality precludes accurate extraction of one or two digits. \Cref{tab:lev_dist} gives the percentage of each Levenshtein distance in cells with errors from the 1956 tables.

Longer strings, or cells where Textract extracts no numeric content at all, contribute to larger distances.

For distances greater than 6, the errors we inspected occur in the disease-name column (column index 0), where Textract extracted a word or word-fragment (e.g.\ ``\,meningococcal'', ``\,infectious'', ``\,epidemic'', ``\,primary'') into a cell the reference transcription records as a plain numeric value or placeholder -- consistent with row misalignment, where a multi-word or multi-row disease-name label bleeds into the row Textract assigned to the adjacent numeric entry. \subsubsection{Detecting Minor Errors with Row Sums}
To mitigate errors from row misalignment during extraction, rows corresponding to the same disease were first grouped from each table. Because similar shifting occurs for the same disease across tables, grouping by disease before checking row sums enforces consistent indexing and ensures quantities are evaluated over the correct entries. \Cref{fig:sumrows} illustrates the data structure: the week's total incidence is in a column titled ``report week'' under ``Canada,'' with contributing cell values appearing every third column for each province and territory.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figure/computing-sums.png}
    \caption{Rows contributing to the computed sums are in blue, and the expected sums are in pink, of the tables in CANDID \cref{sec:NDDdetails}. In the original PDF table, we expect that across a row, the sum of each value in the blue columns equals the total in pink.}
    \label{fig:sumrows}
\end{figure}

For each disease, we plot the difference between Textract's extracted total and the sum calculated by looping over the row contents; \cref{fig:chickenpoxsum} shows the values for Chickenpox.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figure/Chickenpox.pdf}
    \caption{Differences in expected versus calculated row sums for Chickenpox in CANDID (\cref{sec:NDDdetails}) to verify accuracy of cell values (method described in \cref{sec:measuresAccuracy}).}
    \label{fig:chickenpoxsum}
\end{figure}

Most rows show zero difference, indicating agreement between extracted totals and recomputed values; nonzero deviations trace to numerical errors in individual cells.

For example, the first outlier in \cref{fig:chickenpoxsum} is Week 9. Inspecting the scan, the true total is \textit{1224}, from the non-empty contributing values:

\[55,495,379,25,270\]

Textract correctly extracts the total as \textit{1224}, but differs from the reference on the contributing values themselves:

\[50,495,379,25,270\]

Our algorithm sums these to $1,219$, a difference of 5 from the expected total (\cref{fig:chickenpoxsum}): the value 55 was misread as 50. Such minor numerical discrepancies can thus be found and corrected.

Some discrepancies are more subtle. The first outlier in \cref{fig:measlessum} is also Week 9. The recomputed total from the extracted contributing values is:

\[5,13,59,782,802,61,2,47,111\].

Textract reports a total of \textit{1852}, while the sum of the contributing values is \textit{1882} -- a difference of $-30$ (\cref{fig:measlessum}), indicating an error in either the contributing cells or the row total, or both. The reference transcription records the row total as \textit{1862} (\cref{fig:1882}), only 10 away from Textract's \textit{1852} -- not enough to explain the original 30-count gap, leaving a further 20-count gap between the reference total and the sum of its contributing values.

Closer inspection of the source image shows the correct total is \textit{1882} (exactly the sum of the contributing values) -- an error in the reference transcription, not the Textract extraction, arising from scan ambiguity. This is a substantive result in its own right: row-sum validation detected an error in the reference transcription, showing that internal consistency checks can adjudicate between the two sources rather than merely score the extraction tool against an assumed-correct reference (\cref{sec:limitations}).

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.10\linewidth]{figure/1882.png}
    \caption{Total Measles instances in Week 9 of CANDID. This is hard to interpret for both OCR and humans.}
    \label{fig:1882}
\end{figure}

These examples illustrate an advantage of row-sum validation: comparing independently computed totals against reported values flags anomalies in the dataset and can be adapted to detect OCR errors generally.

\subsubsection{Cross-Correlation Between Diseases}

Unless otherwise stated, the Textract series used in this subsection are the processed data described in \cref{tab:pipeline} (Stage 2), without manual correction. The cross-correlation analysis first focuses on Chickenpox and Measles: we extract the relevant time series from both the manually curated and Textract data, then post-process to align formats, handle missing values, and ensure consistent indexing across sources (\cref{sec:postprocessing}).

\Cref{fig:comparisonIncidents} plots both time series for an initial qualitative assessment. Visually, the two series are highly similar, indicating the extraction process preserves the overall timing structure.

This is consistent with \cref{sec:simple}'s content-only agreement of 92.9\% for cells numeric in the manual reference (95.6\% under the numeric-on-both-sides denominator), with most remaining errors involving only small numerical deviations: the dominant trends, seasonal patterns, and relative magnitudes of the two diseases are largely unaffected by extraction noise. The remaining disagreements do change the graphs slightly -- a sharp decrease in Chickenpox around week 60, and missing Measles data around week 145 -- unsurprising given that the Textract data's errors are systematic and sharp; for example, the Scarlet Fever comma-induced shift (\cref{sec:hardcoding}) propagates through all affected rows rather than occurring at random.

For Chickenpox, $r = 0.144$ wk$^{-1}$ from both sources to three significant figures (95\% CI 0.125--0.167 manual, 0.108--0.193 Textract), $\Delta r \approx -0.0004$ wk$^{-1}$; doubling times are 4.8 (4.2, 5.5) and 4.8 (3.6, 6.4) weeks, $\Delta T_{\mathrm{d}} = +0.01$ weeks. In both diseases the Manual and Textract 95\% CIs for $r$ overlap, and each point estimate falls inside the other source's interval; the Chickenpox Textract CI is also visibly wider than its Manual counterpart, expected given no more data points were available to the fit (\crefrange{table:measles_growthrate_results}{tab:growthrateschickenpox} report $n$ observations per window). Overlapping confidence intervals are reported here as a descriptive feature of the two fits, not a statistical test of equivalence; this observation is scoped to the two diseases and two epidemic windows examined here, and does not establish that growth-rate estimation from Textract data is reliable in general.


\subsection{Remarks on Dataset Specific Coding}\label{sec:hardcoding}

Several challenges arose when computing row-wise sums for each disease. The main issue was extraction errors such as row misalignment or mislabeled diseases, which shifted entire rows of values and propagated consistently across tables, making it difficult to associate values with the correct disease. For example, a disease name containing a comma in the original table caused Textract to extract it as two separate cells, shifting the following incidence values; since each table has the same or similar disease names, this problem recurred in every table at that row.

Grouping entries by disease (rather than by week, as extracted) before summation reduced this complexity, limiting the need for manual intervention since most diseases had identical errors across tables. When a shift occurred, the position of the reported total and its contributing values became offset and the indices used to identify them were no longer valid; correct indices were re-identified through manual inspection and the summation inputs adjusted accordingly.

An example in CANDID is Scarlet Fever: persistent discrepancies between reported and recomputed sums, beginning at week 5 of 1956, traced to the disease label changing from ``Scarlet Fever'' to ``Scarlet Fever, etc.'' in the original document. The added comma altered the table parsing, shifting all subsequent cell values in the row and propagating the misalignment through extraction. Resolving this required manually identifying the change and recombining the affected cell values.

A further consideration in the row-sum computation is the structure of the original tables: a row index is printed both at the beginning of each row and repeated at the end (see \cref{fig:NDD1}), and the province ``Report Week'' values are not the only numeric-looking entries in the row. Rather than iterating generically across the row and attempting to filter out non-contributing values at runtime, the row-sum script (\texttt{analysis/row\_sum\_check.py}) avoids this problem by construction: it reads the Canada report-week total from a fixed column and sums only the fixed set of columns, identified directly from the table layout in \cref{fig:NDD1}, that hold each province's report-week value (\cref{fig:hardcode}). Because the contributing columns are specified explicitly rather than inferred from cell content, the row index and any other non-contributing cell are never candidates for inclusion in the sum.

\section{Discussion}


\subsection{Impact}\label{sec:impact}
For epidemiology researchers, automating tabular data entry is a major aid in studying historical data: it accelerates processing large datasets and frees effort for interpretation rather than transcription. Because historical sources are often vast, messy, and inconsistent, automation makes it possible to standardize and analyze them at scale, revealing patterns that would be implausible to uncover manually in comparable time. Our conclusions are limited to printed, regularly formatted CANDID tables from 1956--1958, processed by the specific workflow described above; we do not claim they generalize to handwritten documents, irregularly formatted tables, or other extraction tools or model versions.

For an automation tool to successfully improve and accelerate data entry, it must produce a dataset that is either close to the original, or easy to process and fix. If too much human correction is needed, the time saved becomes small, and data correction is a less pleasant task than data entry even with modest savings. In our preliminary exploration (\hyperref[sec:supp]{Supplementary Material}, S5 Text), the conversational model we tried before Textract produced errors tedious enough to fix that we abandoned it for a tool purpose-built for table extraction.

By comparison, Textract's errors are of a different character than typical manual-entry errors: often either small (one or two characters from the reference, with little influence on downstream statistics) or large. The large-magnitude errors are relatively rare and occur specifically in disease-name string values (\cref{sec:levdist}).

Beyond the initial technical hurdle of using AWS Textract programmatically and the unique post-processing each dataset requires, recreating CANDID with Textract appears to take less overall time and effort than manual data entry. Similar tables (in size and complexity) took on average 30 minutes to fully digitize from a scan by a member of McMaster's Theoretical Biology group; extrapolated at that rate to all 156 tables from 1956--1958, manual entry would take an estimated 78 hours -- an extrapolation from one operator's rate, not a measurement of the full manual process. On the automated side, we directly measured script runtime ($\approx15$ minutes) and the correction pass ($<20$ minutes for the reviewed series, \cref{sec:hardcoding}); script development time and remaining post-processing time were not measured. With these caveats, extraction runtime was short relative to the extrapolated manual-entry estimate, but total person-time was not measured.

Because document-extraction systems evolve rapidly, these findings should be understood as an evaluation of one service and workflow on one historical archive, not a recommendation of Textract for new digitisation projects. Researchers should benchmark currently available tools on representative pages and independently audit both table structure and cell contents before selecting a workflow.



\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{No adjudicated reference standard.} The manual transcription is used throughout as the comparison basis without independent re-adjudication: there was no double transcription or two-reviewer resolution of discordant cells. The paper itself shows this reference can be wrong -- the Week 9 Measles total (\cref{sec:NDD}), where the source scan supports \textit{1882} and the reference records \textit{1862}. Reported disagreement counts therefore conflate extraction and transcription errors, with the direction of bias unknown; resolving this would require two independent reviewers adjudicating a random validation sample against the source scans, which was not done here.

A further limitation is data leakage in developing our normalization and structural-repair rules: the comma/whitespace normalization, header-exclusion, and adjacent-cell tolerance rules in \cref{sec:simple} (\cref{tab:pipeline}) were developed or tuned while the reference transcription was visible to us, with no held-out test set. This also applies to the Scarlet Fever comma fix, the fixed-column row-sum computation, and the Meningitis/Mumps spike correction (all \cref{sec:hardcoding}). Because these rules were shaped by errors observed in this dataset, the reported agreement figures are an upper bound on expected performance on unseen CANDID tables or other datasets, not an estimate of out-of-sample accuracy.

\paragraph{Evaluation year.} Cell-level agreement (\cref{sec:simple}--\cref{sec:levdist}) covers 1956 only (52 of 156 tables); downstream epidemiological statistics (\cref{sec:NDD}) use all three years. Worth naming explicitly: the peak weeks in the growth-rate tables (64, 112, 114, 126) fall entirely outside the range directly measured for cell-level agreement, so their accuracy rests on the untested assumption that agreement in 1957--1958 resembles 1956.

\paragraph{Selection of downstream comparators.} Three disease pairs for cross-correlation and two diseases for growth-rate comparison were chosen without a pre-specified selection rule. A strong cross-correlation near lag zero is expected for two seasonal childhood infections in the same population and is not, on its own, a demanding target; what these comparisons test is whether Textract preserves the \emph{structure} of that dependence rather than establishing a stringent accuracy benchmark.

\paragraph{No external evaluation on a structurally different archive.} All results come from a single source (CANDID) with a consistent printed template; transfer to other table layouts, languages, typographies, or scan qualities is untested. \cref{sec:futurework} discusses a structurally distinct Quebec provincial dataset as a candidate for such a test; we have not analysed it here.

\paragraph{Single tool, single version.} This study evaluates one extraction tool, not a controlled comparison against alternatives; the informal exploration in the Supporting Information (\hyperref[sec:supp]{Supplementary Material}, S5 Text) that led us to Textract was not a rigorous benchmark, and no controlled comparison against other document-extraction tools or current document-capable language models was performed. We also used one commercial service at one point in time, without pinning a specific model or API version; Amazon may change Textract's underlying models without notice, so results reported here may not reproduce exactly if re-extracted today. Our choice of Textract reflects its suitability for this task, not a demonstrated advantage over alternatives.

The growth-rate comparison (\crefrange{table:measles_growthrate_results}{tab:growthrateschickenpox}) is similarly limited: only the Stage-2 processed series, without manual spike correction, is available for Measles and Chickenpox, since manual correction (\cref{sec:hardcoding}) was applied only to Meningitis and Mumps. We are therefore unable to test whether the growth-rate estimate is stable across raw, processed, and manually corrected versions of the same disease's data; doing so would require applying the same manual review to Measles and Chickenpox, which was not done here.

\paragraph{No measured time-and-cost study.} The labour comparison in \cref{sec:impact} is an extrapolation from one operator's rate, not a controlled measurement: script development time, dataset-specific coding time, validation time, retries, and AWS charges were not measured, and only extraction runtime ($\approx15$ minutes) and one correction pass ($<20$ minutes, two series) were timed directly. A defensible comparison would record person-hours by activity, operator count and experience, machine time, itemised AWS charges with a pricing date, and would separate first-project setup cost from the marginal cost of digitizing an additional year of tables.

\bigskip
\noindent The limitations above concern the validity and scope of what was measured. The remainder of this section concerns the operational cost of using Textract -- not the validity of the results, but relevant to a researcher deciding whether to adopt this workflow.

The main operational limitation is the barrier to entry of AWS software: limited development experience may increase the effort required. Beyond account set-up and writing the extraction script, connecting to an AWS account involves an AWS ID, secret key, secret access key, and more (\hyperref[sec:supp]{Supplementary Material}, S1). Once set up, though, researchers have complete control over how data is processed and extracted -- e.g.\ omitting confidence levels, or processing only certain table elements or pages -- and the same extraction script works for any scanned table document, unlike the data-specific post-processing that follows.

Another operational limitation is extraction time: extracting the full 156-table CANDID dataset took around 15 minutes. This could likely be reduced, e.g.\ by cropping scans to the relevant table region or omitting confidence levels, but optimizing API calls was not a priority here.

The largest operational limitation is the time and effort to process and clean data post-extraction: formatting errors (merged cells, shifted rows) and large errors in rare numeric values and string headers together took more time to find and correct than extraction itself. Small errors, present throughout, can be particularly hard to detect when they occur in numerical values that appear plausible on their own, and often do not stand out unless validated against source data.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:futurework}

Future trials on different datasets will reveal more about the power and limitations of tools such as Textract, expanding to more complicated tables and handwritten documents. One natural next test case is a Quebec provincial communicable-disease dataset (Dominion Bureau of Statistics, July 1927--December 1931), collected by McMaster's Theoretical Biology group but not previously digitized or transcribed. It is structurally distinct from CANDID in ways that would stress-test this workflow: row headers printed diagonally and in French, and a table layout that changes partway through the series from two tables per page to one. We have not analysed this dataset here -- doing so properly would require an independent manual transcription of a stratified sample, beyond this paper's scope -- but it is a natural candidate for validating the workflow's generalizability beyond a single archive.

Our results suggest that even when initial extraction performs reasonably well, the bulk of human effort remains in post-extraction processing: cleaning inconsistencies, correcting misread values, aligning rows and columns, and validating outputs against source documents. The main bottleneck is not extraction itself, but the downstream effort to make the data usable.

A key objective going forward is determining how much of this post-processing can be automated. Machine learning tools have strong potential here: natural-language errors such as mislabeled headers (producing high Levenshtein distances) could be identified and corrected using domain-tuned LLMs, and more sophisticated validation software could flag anomalies such as outliers or structural irregularities automatically -- either correcting errors directly or generating targeted reports for human review, reducing manual workload while improving consistency and scalability.

\subsection{Concluding Remarks}

Taken together, this evaluation indicates that Textract, combined with the dataset-specific post-processing workflow described here, reproduced the manual reference transcription closely enough at the cell level, and preserved the downstream epidemiological quantities closely enough, to be a plausible option for this archive. It does not establish that the same workflow -- or Textract generally -- would perform comparably on other historical tabular sources. Researchers considering a similar workflow should treat these figures as a case study rather than a benchmark, and independently audit both extraction accuracy and downstream statistical quantities on their own material before relying on an automated extraction pipeline.

\noindent\textbf{S3 Text. Textract JSON block structure.}

At this point in the API call (\cref{sec:data-extraction}), Textract does not yet know ``rows'' or ``columns''; it only sees shapes and text. Textract returns a flat list of \texttt{BLOCK} objects (not a table as recognized by humans). The response is one JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) array called \texttt{Blocks}. Each block contains, among other information, \texttt{BlockType}, which contains values such as \texttt{TABLE, CELL, WORD}, and \texttt{SELECTION\_ELEMENT}. Each block also contains a confidence score.

A \texttt{BlockType} of ``TABLE'' represents one detected table and contains a list of cell ids. Each \texttt{CELL} block contains \texttt{RowIndex, ColumnIndex, RowSpan/ColumnSpan}, and relationships to \texttt{WORD} blocks, which contain the actual string value of the cell along with its own confidence value.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{S4 Text. Levenshtein distance recurrence.}

Let $s = s_1 s_2 \dots s_m$ and $t = t_1 t_2 \dots t_n$ be two strings of lengths $m$ and $n$. The Levenshtein distance $D(i,j)$ between the characters $s_1 \dots s_i$ and $t_1 \dots t_j$ is defined by the standard dynamic-programming recurrence \cite{wagner1974string}:
\[
D(0,j) = j \quad \text{for } j = 0,1,\dots,n
\]
\[
D(i,0) = i \quad \text{for } i = 0,1,\dots,m
\]
\[
D(i,j) =
\min \begin{cases}
D(i-1,j) + 1 & \text{(deletion)} \\
D(i,j-1) + 1 & \text{(insertion)} \\
D(i-1,j-1) + \mathbf{1}_{s_i \neq t_j} & \text{(substitution)}
\end{cases}
\]
\[
\mathbf{1}_{s_i \neq t_j} =
\begin{cases}
0, & s_i = t_j \\
1, & s_i \neq t_j
\end{cases}
\]
The Levenshtein distance between strings $s$ and $t$ is then $\operatorname{Lev}(s,t) = D(m,n)$.

\noindent\textbf{S5 Text. Preliminary exploration of a conversational LLM for table extraction.}\label{sec:chatgpt-pilot}

Before settling on Textract, we informally tried extracting a small number of CANDID tables by prompting a general-purpose conversational large language model (ChatGPT) to read the scanned table and return its contents \cite{stewart}. This was an unstructured, non-benchmarked pilot on a handful of tables rather than a controlled comparison: we did not evaluate it against a reference transcription, vary prompts systematically, or record error rates.
